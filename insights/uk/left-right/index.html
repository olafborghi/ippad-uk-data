Left, right and the centre

Ideology
Age
Gender
Where young people in the UK place themselves on the left-right scale, and how this differs by age and gender.
Published

24 November 2026

Left and right remain the most common shorthand for political positions, but it is not obvious that young people, and adolescents in particular, think about politics in these terms. In all four surveys, respondents placed themselves on a scale from 0 (left) to 10 (right), and we look at how their answers differ with age and between young women and young men.

Most answers are close to the centre

Each panel shows one survey. The bars give the share of respondents who chose each point of the scale.

Across the four surveys, 49% of respondents placed themselves between 4 and 6, and the exact middle was by far the most common answer. This was particularly true of the youngest respondents. Among those aged 12 to 15, 38% chose the midpoint, compared with 29% among those aged 18 and over. For some, the middle will reflect a genuinely moderate position. For others, it may indicate that they do not (yet) think about politics in terms of left and right, so averages on this scale should be read with care.

The average positions in Gallup W1 (4.4), Gallup W2 (5.0) and Survation W2 (5.0) are close to each other, whereas Survation W1 stands out at 6.1. We do not think this reflects a real shift to the right among younger adolescents. Respondents in Survation W1 gave higher answers on all scales from 0 to 10, and those who took part again a year later placed themselves much like respondents in the other surveys. The difference thus seems to come from who took part rather than from what young people of that age think (see The data).

Age

Each dot is the average position at one year of age in one survey, with a line for its 95% confidence interval. Ages with fewer than 20 respondents in a survey are left out.

Within Gallup W1 and the two Survation surveys, the average position hardly changes with age. The clearest pattern appears in Gallup W2, the only survey that covers the full range from 16 to 26, where the average rises from 4.0 among 16 and 17 year olds to 4.5 among 18 to 21 year olds and 5.5 among those aged 22 to 26. A single survey cannot tell whether this reflects young people moving to the right as they grow older or differences between generations. Because the surveys cover different age ranges, a curve drawn across all ages from 12 to 26 would also mix differences between ages with differences between surveys.

Gender

Each row compares the average position of young women and young men in one age group of one survey. A longer line means a larger gap. Groups with fewer than 20 respondents are left out.

The gender difference is the most consistent pattern in these data. In every survey and age group, young women place themselves further left than young men. However, the size of the gap changes with age. Pooled over the surveys, it is only 0.2 points among 12 to 15 year olds, but grows to 0.6 points among 16 and 17 year olds and 0.8 points among 18 to 21 year olds, before narrowing slightly to 0.5 points among those aged 22 to 26. The gap thus seems to open up in the late teens, a period that research on political socialisation regards as formative for political attitudes.

Left and right, progressive and conservative

Three of the four surveys also asked respondents to place themselves between socially progressive (0) and socially conservative (10). The two scales correlate at 0.48, so young people who see themselves on the left also tend to describe themselves as progressive. However, the relationship is far from perfect, which suggests that for many young people social values and left-right position do not fully coincide. You can compare both scales by age, gender and survey in the explorer.

The samples are not weighted and the surveys differ in age range and recruitment, so we compare groups within the same survey where possible. The data page describes how the surveys differ.
