Interest, democracy and trust

How interested young people in the UK are in politics, how satisfied they are with democracy, and how much they trust its institutions.
Published

24 November 2026

How young people relate to democracy matters for research on political development, because attitudes formed in these years can last. All four surveys asked about political interest and satisfaction with democracy, and three of them asked about trust in five institutions. Because the surveys cover different ages, we compare age groups mostly within a survey.

Interest in politics

Each line shows one survey. The dots give the share of respondents at each year of age who are fairly or very interested in politics. Ages with fewer than 20 respondents in a survey are left out.

In Gallup W2, the only survey that covers ages 16 to 26, the share who are fairly or very interested in politics rises from 38% at age 16 to 75% at age 26. Gallup W1 shows a similar rise between 16 and 21. Within the Survation surveys, by contrast, interest differs less between 12 and 17 year olds. The two Survation surveys also differ from each other, as 57% of respondents in W1 but only 43% in W2 are fairly or very interested. Interestingly, the 72 Survation W1 respondents who took part again a year later were less interested in W1 (39%) than the other W1 respondents (59%). As with the higher answers of Survation W1 on 0 to 10 scales, this suggests that the difference between the waves partly reflects who took part. Taking all ages of a survey together, young men say slightly more often than young women that they are interested in politics, by 4 to 6 percentage points.

Satisfaction with democracy

Each bar shows the answers of young women or young men in one survey to the statement “I am satisfied with the way that democracy works in the United Kingdom”, on a scale from 1 (fully disagree) to 7 (fully agree).

In both Gallup surveys, more young people are dissatisfied with the way democracy works in the UK than satisfied (48% against 30% in W1, and 45% against 33% in W2). Among the adolescents in Survation, satisfaction is more common. In Survation W1, more respondents are satisfied than dissatisfied, while in Survation W2 the two groups are of similar size and 35% choose the middle of the scale. In every survey, young women are satisfied less often than young men, for instance 29% against 38% in Gallup W2. Within Gallup W2, satisfaction is also more common among 22 to 26 year olds (40%) than among 18 to 21 year olds (27%). Satisfaction also goes together with interest. Across the surveys, 56% of those who are very interested in politics are satisfied with democracy, compared with 13% of those who are not at all interested.

Trust in institutions

Each row is one institution, and the dots give its average trust rating in each survey on a scale from 0 (no trust at all) to 10 (complete trust). Survation W2 did not ask these questions.

In all three surveys that asked about trust, the courts and the police are trusted most, while political parties and the media are trusted least. In Gallup W1, for example, trust averages 4.9 for the police but 3.7 for political parties. Survation W1 shows higher trust in every institution, which again fits its higher answers on all 0 to 10 scales. The two Gallup surveys invite a comparison over time, since Gallup W1 was fielded under a Conservative government and Gallup W2 under a Labour government. However, among 18 to 21 year olds, the one age group that both surveys cover well, trust in the UK government is almost the same (3.7 and 3.9). The higher average of Gallup W2 thus comes mainly from its 22 to 26 year olds, who trust the government more (5.0). In both Gallup surveys, young men also trust the government somewhat more than young women, with averages of 4.7 and 4.2 in Gallup W2.

Ways of governing

Each row is one way of governing a country. The dots give the share of respondents in each survey who find it fairly or very good (5 to 7 on a scale from 1, very bad, to 7, very good).

Large majorities in all surveys find democratic ways of governing good, and elections where every vote counts equally receive the most support, from 69% in Gallup W1 to 90% in Survation W1. At the same time, a strong leader who does not accept interference from anyone is rated good by 40% in Gallup W1, 48% in Gallup W2 and close to three fifths in both Survation surveys. These views are not mutually exclusive. In Survation W2, for example, 52% of respondents rate both equal elections and a strong leader as good. This could mean that some young people read “strong leader” as effective leadership rather than as a rejection of democratic checks, a possibility that these data cannot settle.

About these data

The four surveys differ in age range, recruitment and timing, and the data are not weighted. Survation W1 respondents give higher answers on 0 to 10 scales, which affects the trust items, and Survation W2 did not ask about trust. The data page explains the design.
